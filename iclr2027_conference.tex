\title{What a Policy Leaves Behind: A Controlled Study of Zero-Shot Cross-Embodiment Transfer with Camera-Frame Actions}

\author{Anonymous authors\\
Paper under double-blind review}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Zero-shot transfer of vision-language-action policies to robots absent from training is central to generalisable manipulation, and the literature reports it under two protocols that answer different questions: one keeps the target robot away from all training, the other lets it appear during pretraining and only withholds it from task-specific post-training. We separate the two protocols and study zero-shot cross-embodiment transfer for stationary tabletop manipulation with fixed-base arms and two-finger grippers as a controlled experiment around one principle: a policy transfers to the extent that what it reads and writes carries nothing that belongs to the source robots alone. Four channels carry such source specificity, the frame of the state-action representation, the diversity of the source family, the auxiliary supervision and the exposure of the target during pretraining, and each becomes one research question under one backbone, one pretraining budget of 48K trajectories and one evaluation. The benchmark pairs a procedurally generated family of 128 Franka-type source embodiments with eight commercially used targets held out from all training and grouped into appearance, gripper, arm and full changes, with cameras aligned in a shared tabletop frame so that image differences come from the robot alone. On the representation channel we extend the hierarchy of frames from the base and the gripper to the camera: actions are described by their motion effects, the image-plane displacements of the gripper keypoints from anchors that follow analytically from the kinematics of the robot in hand, and a new robot decodes commands from the predicted effect through a closed-form geometric solve. Under the strict protocol, gripper-frame states and actions raise average task progress by eighteen points over base-frame variants, camera-frame effects add a further 6.6 points on average and 12.9 on full changes, source diversity under a fixed budget adds sixteen points and effect supervision at eight sources matches gripper-frame commands at 128, image-grounded auxiliary targets help most, and target exposure during pretraining raises every representation while narrowing the gaps between them. The effect error on a target predicts its transfer before any task rollout, the removed failures concentrate in the grasp phase, and the recipe keeps its value on established single-robot benchmarks. The whole study fits sixteen GPUs.
\end{abstract}
