\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A robot policy trained on one arm should keep working when the arm changes. Hardware evolves faster than task-specific data can be collected, and every new gripper or manipulator restarts the collection effort when the policy has to be retrained from scratch. Vision-language-action models promise a way out by pretraining on many robots and transferring to new ones. The promise rests on a question that the literature answers inconsistently: what does the policy output, and in which frame is that output expressed.

Two observations motivate this paper. The first concerns protocol. Reports of zero-shot transfer to a new robot fall into two groups that differ in one condition. In one group the target robot is absent from every stage of training. In the other group the target robot appears during pretraining and is only absent from task-specific post-training. The two settings answer different questions, and treating them as one hides the effect of the representation behind the effect of data composition. The second observation concerns frames. A motion of the gripper can be described relative to the robot base, relative to the gripper itself, or relative to the camera that observes it. The base is a convention of the manufacturer. The gripper frame is shared by every two-finger robot up to the geometry of its fingers. The camera is the one frame that every embodiment in a matched setup shares exactly, because the image is the channel through which the policy perceives the world. Moving the action from the base to the gripper is known to help transfer. Moving it further, into the camera, is the step this paper takes.

We describe actions by their effects: the motion that the fingertips and pads of the gripper undergo in the image over the next fraction of a second. The starting position of every keypoint follows analytically from the state of the robot and its kinematics, so the policy predicts only the displacement from that anchor. A new robot brings its own keypoints and its own kinematics, and the same predicted displacement is translated into a command for that robot by solving a small geometric problem at the output. Embodiment enters at the input boundary through the anchors and at the output boundary through the decoding, and the learned mapping in between is free of any convention of the source robot. The same quantity serves as an auxiliary target for a policy that still emits gripper-frame commands, and in that role it shapes the features of the policy toward the geometry of the interaction.

To measure the value of the camera frame we build a controlled benchmark in simulation in which everything except the robot is held fixed. A procedurally generated family of 128 Franka-type arms with varied link lengths, finger shapes and colours serves as the source for pretraining under a fixed budget of 48K trajectories. Eight commercially used embodiments, all fixed-base arms with two-finger grippers, serve as targets that are held out from all training and grouped by what changes: appearance, gripper, arm, or both. Cameras are aligned in a shared tabletop frame so that image differences come from the robot itself. Post-training uses only the source Franka on three downstream tasks, and evaluation scores task progress on every target with thousands of rollouts per model. The compute budget of the whole study fits sixteen GPUs over three weeks, which keeps the design within reach of an academic group.

Under this protocol the frame of the action orders the outcomes. Relative gripper-frame states and actions raise average progress over base-frame variants by eighteen points. Decoding commands from predicted camera-frame effects adds another two points on average and five points on full embodiment changes. Effect prediction as an auxiliary target adds seven points on average and thirteen on full changes, ahead of language, waypoint and object-box targets. The gain persists at every level of source diversity and is largest when the family is least diverse, to the point that eight sources with effect prediction match 128 sources trained on gripper-frame commands alone. Seeing the target during pretraining raises every representation and narrows the gaps between them, which confirms that the two protocols must be reported apart. The prediction error of the effect on a target orders the targets by their transfer, and the failures that effect prediction removes are the grasp-phase failures in which the geometry of the gripper decides the outcome. The same recipe carries over to established benchmarks and to physically different arms.

Our contributions are the following. We formulate camera-frame motion effects as an action representation whose embodiment dependence is confined to analytic anchoring and decoding, together with a training objective and a decoder that make it executable on an unseen gripper. We present a controlled benchmark that separates the target-absent and target-seen protocols and isolates appearance, gripper, arm and full changes among fixed-base two-finger manipulators at an academic compute scale. We provide a factorised study of frames, source diversity, auxiliary targets and target exposure, and a mechanism analysis that explains where and why the camera frame helps.
